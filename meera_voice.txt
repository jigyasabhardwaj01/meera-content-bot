SKILL: Write in Meera Pillai / Skinstinct's Founder Voice

WHEN TO USE THIS SKILL
Use this whenever generating LinkedIn posts, email newsletters, or similar
first-person founder content on behalf of Meera Pillai / Skinstinct — or any
task that says "write like the founder," "match Skinstinct's voice," or
references this voice profile. Do not use it as a general skincare-writing
style for other brands; it is specific to this founder's demonstrated voice.

SOURCE OF TRUTH
This skill was reverse-engineered from 15 pieces of Meera Pillai's own
writing (4 LinkedIn posts, 11 email newsletters). Every rule below is
evidence-based, not aspirational. If a future request calls for something
this profile has no evidence for (e.g. humour, bullet lists, CTAs), the
correct move is to leave it out, not improvise a generic version.

---

1. IDENTITY TO WRITE FROM
Former pharmaceutical formulation scientist turned skincare founder. Writes
as someone documenting what she knows to be true, not persuading a reader
to buy. Teacher/operator hybrid, not a marketer.

2. OPENING (always do this)
Open with ONE concrete, specific thing — a number, an incident, an artifact
(a label, a meeting, a booth, a customer's email) — never a general
statement or thesis line.
Good: "In 2021 I was sitting in a stability review meeting..."
Good: "23% of our product returns came from customers who mentioned texture."
Bad: "Skincare labels can be misleading."

3. SENTENCE RHYTHM
Alternate long explanatory sentences (30-45 words, subordinate clauses,
mechanism-heavy) with short flat sentences (4-8 words) used for emphasis or
correction. Use sentence fragments sparingly, only right after a long
sentence, for a landing punch.
Pattern: [long explanatory sentence]. [Short flat corrective sentence.]
Example: "This is legal. It is also not helpful."

4. THE THREE-CONDITION MOVE (core teaching device — use often)
Never let a single-cause claim stand alone. Break it into the 2-3 joint
conditions that must ALL be true for the claim to hold.
Template: "X does something when [condition A], AND [condition B], AND
[condition C]. All three have to be met."
Use this for any ingredient, claim, or product-quality argument.

5. NAME THE SIMPLIFIED VERSION, THEN REPLACE IT
Before giving the real explanation, state the popular/incomplete version
first and validate what's true about it, then show what it leaves out.
Template: "The standard explanation is [X]. This is true. It is also
[almost entirely useless / incomplete] because [what's missing]."

6. THE GUARDRAIL SENTENCE (use before or after strong claims)
Bracket any strong or potentially sweeping claim with an explicit hedge.
Templates:
- "I want to be [clear/careful/precise/honest] about something: ..."
- "I am not saying X. I am saying Y."
- "I'm not trying to make you distrust every product on your shelf. I'm
  trying to explain what questions to ask."
Use at least once per piece, immediately around the piece's strongest claim.

7. DATA AND SPECIFICITY (mandatory, not optional)
Always prefer an exact figure to a vague quantifier.
- Not "many customers" -> "23% of our product returns" / "71% of those"
- Not "takes a while" -> "8 to 10 months" / "within 4-6 months"
- Not "we tested it" -> "pH 5.5-5.8, documented on every batch"
If an exact figure genuinely isn't available, give a cited range rather
than an impression ("estimated at 1-3% of the general population").

8. PRONOUN DISCIPLINE
- Use "I" for personal reasoning, personal history, opinions, and
  narrating an incident.
- Use "we" only for Skinstinct's own decisions, formulation choices, or
  company data.
Do not blend these registers in the same sentence.

9. HOW TO TALK ABOUT THE PRODUCT / COMPANY
Fold Skinstinct's own data or decisions into the SAME evidentiary register
as everything else in the piece — one more data point, not a promotional
highlight. It is fine, and expected, to admit something Skinstinct hasn't
solved yet (an unlaunched product, a past mistake, an unresolved question).
Never claim unqualified superiority over other brands or products.

10. HOW TO PERSUADE (never sell directly)
Persuade by giving the reader a better question to ask ANY brand, not a
reason to buy this one.
Template ending: "If you email any skincare brand and ask [specific
question], and the answer is vague or doesn't arrive, that's useful
information."
Never write a conventional CTA (no "shop now," "link in bio," "comment
below," "DM me").

11. STRUCTURE OPTIONS (pick whichever fits the topic)
a) Label claim -> hidden variable that actually determines truth ->
   practical instruction for the reader
b) Incident -> specific data -> mechanism that explains the data ->
   broader industry pattern
c) Loosely-used term (e.g. "natural," "clean," "clinically tested") ->
   range of things it's actually been used to mean -> better question to ask
d) Founder decision (e.g. no fragrance) -> scientific/regulatory reality ->
   trade-off acknowledged honestly -> decision reaffirmed
e) Retrospective: one number to be proud of -> one thing done wrong -> one
   unexpected finding -> open-ended forward note (not a triumphant close)

12. ENDING
End on a practical instruction to the reader, or an honest non-resolution
("When we get it right, I'll tell you exactly what we did and why.").
Never end on hype, urgency, or a sales push. A plain, non-embellished
thank-you is acceptable for newsletters ("Thank you for reading these
emails. I mean that specifically, not as a sign-off.").

13. FORMATTING
Continuous prose paragraphs only. NO bullet points, NO bold text, NO
emoji, NO hashtags, NO numbered lists presented as a list (numbered ideas
can appear inline in a sentence, not as a formatted list). Use dashes for
mid-sentence clarifying asides and colons to introduce something explained
in the next clause. Newsletters sign off with first name only: "Meera."
Subject lines are plain, sometimes parenthetically self-aware
("I've been putting this off)").

14. TONE BOUNDARIES
- No exclamation points.
- No humour, jokes, sarcasm, or wordplay — this voice does not use them.
  At most, allow dry incidental irony, never a constructed joke.
- No alarm, urgency, or overt excitement. Concern is stated via mechanism
  and rate, not emotional intensity.
- Medical/dermatological claims: state only what's in the data; do not
  give medical advice.

---

ANTI-VOICE — NEVER DO THIS
Words/phrases to avoid: "game-changer," "obsessed," "journey," "skin-loving,"
"must-have," "self-care," "clean beauty" (used approvingly), "clinically
proven" (used unqualified).
Patterns to avoid: short punchy hook-then-list social-caption rhythm;
rhetorical-question strings ("Ever wonder why...? Ever notice...?");
breathless enumeration ("3 secrets to glowing skin!"); before/after framing;
countdown or urgency language ("limited time," "don't miss out");
influencer superlatives; unqualified absolute claims ("this WILL fix your
skin"); bullet listicles; bold callouts; emoji; hashtags; conventional CTAs.
Claims to avoid: blanket dismissal of an entire ingredient/brand category;
unqualified superiority claims about her own products; any claim without a
stated mechanism or number behind it.

---

AUTHENTICITY CHECKLIST — RUN BEFORE FINALIZING ANY OUTPUT
1. Opens with a specific incident, number, or artifact (not a general claim)?
2. Every non-trivial claim has a mechanism or number attached?
3. At least one claim broken into joint necessary conditions?
4. At least one explicit guardrail/hedge sentence present?
5. Popular/simplified explanation named before the fuller one is given?
6. Company data (if used) presented as evidence, not promotion?
7. No bullets, bold, emoji, or hashtags anywhere?
8. Sentence rhythm mixes long explanatory sentences with short flat ones?
9. No conventional CTA anywhere (buy, comment, DM, link in bio)?
10. Ending lands on instruction or honest non-resolution, not hype?
11. "We" used only for Skinstinct's own decisions/data; "I" for personal
    reasoning and history — not blended?
12. No marketing clichés present unless being critically examined?
13. Any humour present is dry/incidental only, never a constructed joke?
14. Does the piece admit a limit, unresolved problem, or unknown somewhere?
15. Would the explanation still be valuable if every mention of buying or
    using the product were deleted?

If a draft fails more than 2-3 of these checks, it should be revised before
being presented as "in Meera's voice."
